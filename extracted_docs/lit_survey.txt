2. Related Works
Taslimi et al. (2022), in the paper titled “ SwinCheX : Multi-label Classification on Chest X-Ray Images with Transformers”, propose a transformer-based framework for automated thoracic disease detection using hierarchical Vision Transformers. The primary objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of Swin Transformers for multi-label chest radiograph classification and to establish a fair evaluation protocol on the ChestX-ray14 dataset. Unlike conventional CNN-based approaches such as DenseNet and ResNet , the proposed method utilizes a Swin-L Transformer backbone with shifted-window multi-head self-attention to model long-range dependencies efficiently while maintaining linear computational complexity. The architecture consists of a shared Swin Transformer encoder followed by pathology-specific multi-layer perceptron (MLP) heads under a multi-task learning paradigm. The 3-layer head configuration (384–48–48 neurons) demonstrated optimal performance. Binary cross-entropy loss is used for training, with ImageNet-22K pretrained weights for initialization. Experiments are conducted on the NIH ChestX-ray14 dataset containing 112,120 frontal/back-view X-ray images across 14 disease classes, using the official patient-wise split to prevent data leakage. The primary evaluation metric is AUC-ROC. The proposed 3-layer SwinCheX model achieves a state-of-the-art mean AUC of 0.810, surpassing the previous DenseNet -based DNet model (0.807 AUC). It achieves superior performance in 11 out of 14 pathologies and demonstrates improved localization capability using Grad-CAM visualizations, confirming attention to clinically relevant lung regions. Despite strong classification performance, limitations include absence of imbalance-specific loss handling, no explicit modeling of long-tailed distributions, and lack of multimodal (vision-language) integration for semantic reasoning. [1]
Khan et al. (2025), in the paper titled “ ChestGPT : Integrating Large Language Models and Vision Transformers for Disease Detection and Localization in Chest X-Rays”, propose a unified vision-language framework that jointly performs disease classification and spatial localization in chest radiographs. The primary objective is to bridge the gap between pure vision-based diagnostic models and multimodal reasoning systems by integrating the EVA Vision Transformer ( ViT ) with the LLaMA 2 large language model. In the proposed architecture, chest X-ray images are first partitioned into patches and processed by a frozen EVA ViT backbone to extract visual feature embeddings. These embeddings are projected via a trainable linear layer into token representations, which are then concatenated with carefully engineered radiology-specific prompts and fed into the LLaMA 2 model for joint classification and bounding-box generation. The framework leverages transfer learning with initialization from MiniGPT-v2 and MiniGPT -Med, and applies prompt engineering strategies including default, global, and local prompting styles to enhance interpretability and task performance. Experiments are conducted on the VinDr -CXR dataset comprising 18,000 chest X-rays annotated with 6 global disease labels and 22 local pathology labels with bounding boxes. Evaluation metrics include Accuracy, Micro-F1, Macro-F1 for classification; Intersection over Union ( IoU ) for localization; and BLEU and ROUGE-1 for textual validity. The best-performing configuration ( MiniGPT -Med initialization with batch size 10) achieved 0.75 classification accuracy and 0.76 Micro-F1 for global disease prediction. For localization, Top-1 IoU reached 0.31 under local prompting, demonstrating spatial grounding capability. Text generation quality achieved ROUGE-1 up to 0.74 and BLEU score of 0.23. While the model demonstrates strong multimodal reasoning and explainability, limitations include hallucinated abnormalities, moderate localization accuracy, dependency on prompt formulation, and potential overfitting to frequent “No Finding” cases. [2]
Monajatipoor et al. (2021), in the paper titled “ BERTHop : An Effective Vision-and-Language Model for Chest X-ray Disease Diagnosis”, propose a data-efficient transformer-based vision-language (V&amp;L) framework specifically designed to address the domain gap between general-domain multimodal pretraining and medical imaging applications. The primary objective is to improve thoracic disease diagnosis by jointly modeling chest X-ray images and radiology reports while avoiding reliance on large-scale general-domain V&amp;L pretraining. The proposed BERTHop architecture integrates PixelHop ++ as an unsupervised visual feature extractor with a single-stream transformer initialized using BlueBERT , a biomedical-domain pretrained language model. PixelHop ++ extracts multi-frequency image representations via successive subspace learning without backpropagation, followed by PCA-based dimensionality reduction and feature concatenation. These visual embeddings are fused with report embeddings within a transformer framework to capture cross-modal associations. The model is trained and evaluated on the OpenI dataset comprising 3,684 image-text pairs across 14 thoracic disease classes, using AUC-ROC as the evaluation metric due to dataset imbalance. Experimental results reported in Table 1 (Page 7) show that BERTHop achieves an average AUC of 98.23%, outperforming TieNet (96.5%), TNNT (85.4%), and VisualBERT with BUTD (83.86%). Notably, BERTHop surpasses TieNet by 1.73% while being trained on a dataset approximately nine times smaller. Per-class AUC values include 0.9967 for Pneumonia, 1.0000 for Pneumothorax, and 0.9896 for Cardiomegaly. Ablation studies in Table 2 demonstrate that BlueBERT initialization improves performance by 6.46% over standard BERT when paired with PixelHop ++. Visual encoder comparison in Table 3 confirms PixelHop ++ (0.9823 avg AUC) significantly outperforms BUTD (0.8564) and ChexNet (0.8798). Despite strong performance and data efficiency, limitations include dependence on paired image-text data, absence of explicit imbalance-aware loss strategies, and lack of long-tailed optimization mechanisms for rare thoracic conditions. [3]
Thawkar et al. (2023), in the paper titled “ XrayGPT : Chest Radiographs Summarization using Large Medical Vision-Language Models”, propose a conversational medical vision-language model designed to generate interactive radiology summaries and answer open-ended clinical queries from chest radiographs. The primary objective is to align a domain-specific medical visual encoder with a fine-tuned large language model (LLM) to enable radiology-aware summarization and dialogue grounded in chest X-ray findings. The proposed XrayGPT architecture integrates a frozen MedCLIP visual encoder with a Vicuna-based LLM through a learnable linear projection layer for cross-modal alignment. The model follows a two-stage training strategy: Stage-1 training uses 213,514 high-quality interactive summaries derived from MIMIC-CXR reports, while Stage-2 fine-tunes on 3,403 curated summaries from OpenI to improve robustness and reduce noise. A total of approximately 217k cleaned interactive summaries are generated through structured preprocessing and GPT-3.5-assisted refinement to enhance domain specificity. The model is evaluated using ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L metrics on the MIMIC-CXR test set. Quantitative results reported in Table 1 (Page 5) demonstrate progressive improvements over the MiniGPT-4 baseline. The baseline achieves ROUGE-1 = 0.1313, while integrating MedCLIP increases it to 0.1517. Further incorporating MedVicuna improves ROUGE-1 to 0.2099, and the full XrayGPT model with RadVicuna achieves ROUGE-1 = 0.3213, ROUGE-2 = 0.0912, and ROUGE-L = 0.1997, representing an absolute improvement of 19% in ROUGE-1 over the baseline. GPT-based evaluation further indicates that 82% of generated responses were preferred over baseline outputs. Despite strong conversational and summarization performance, limitations include heavy reliance on large-scale instruction tuning, absence of explicit disease classification benchmarking, lack of imbalance-aware optimization, and dependency on generative evaluation metrics rather than clinical detection metrics such as AUC or F1-score. [4]
Lee et al. (2025), in the paper titled “CXR- LLaVA : A Multimodal Large Language Model for Interpreting Chest X-ray Images”, propose an open-source multimodal large language model (LLM) designed for automated chest X-ray (CXR) interpretation and radiologic report generation. The primary objective is to adapt the LLaVA architecture for domain-specific radiology applications by integrating a custom-trained Vision Transformer ( ViT -L/16) image encoder with the LLaMA-2 (7B) language model. Unlike general-purpose multimodal LLMs, the proposed framework employs a two-step image encoder training strategy: (1) supervised binary classification pretraining (normal vs. abnormal) using 374,881 labeled CXRs, and (2) CLIP-based contrastive alignment between image and pathology text labels using 592,580 image-text pairs. Subsequently, a projection layer aligns image features with the LLaMA-2 embedding space, followed by instruction tuning using 217,699 radiology report pairs and GPT-4-refined multi-turn question–answer data. The model was evaluated on internal (MIMIC, CheXpert ) and external (Indiana University) test sets. On the MIMIC internal test set (Table 2, page 7), CXR- LLaVA achieved an average F1 score of 0.81, sensitivity of 0.80, and specificity of 0.89 across six major pathologies, outperforming GPT-4-Vision (F1 = 0.62) and Gemini-Pro-Vision (F1 = 0.68). On the CheXpert internal test set (Table 3, page 8), it achieved an average F1 score of 0.57, slightly below CheXzero (0.61) and human radiologists (0.62). On the Indiana external test set (Table 4, page 10), the model obtained an overall F1 score of 0.56, with strong performance for cardiomegaly (0.62) and edema (0.67), but poor detection of pneumothorax (0.05). Human evaluation (Table 5, page 12) showed a 72.7% autonomous reporting success rate (acceptable without or with minor revision), compared to 84.0% for ground truth reports (p &lt; 0.001). While the model demonstrates strong multimodal reasoning and report generation capability, limitations include reduced sensitivity for subtle lesions (e.g., pneumothorax), hallucination risks inherent to LLMs, exclusion of support device descriptions, and lower performance compared to radiologists in challenging cases. [5]
Singh and Singh (2025), in the paper titled “ ChestX -Transcribe: A Multimodal Transformer for Automated Radiology Report Generation from Chest X-Rays”, propose a sequence-to-sequence multimodal transformer framework designed for automated radiology report generation. The primary objective is to reduce radiologists’ workload by generating clinically coherent and semantically rich reports from chest radiographs using a vision-language architecture. The proposed model integrates a hierarchical Swin Transformer as the visual encoder and DistilGPT (12-layer transformer) as the language decoder. The Swin Transformer extracts high-resolution image features through patch partitioning, window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA), shifted-window attention (SW-MSA), and patch merging. A projection layer is introduced to align the 768-dimensional visual embeddings with the input space of the DistilGPT language model, enabling effective cross-modal fusion. The model is trained on the Indiana University Chest X-ray dataset containing 7,430 images from 3,825 patients, with a total of 155,837 tokens across reports. Training is conducted for 5 epochs using the Adam optimizer with differential learning rates (2e-4 for language model, 5e-5 for projection layer). Quantitative results reported in Table 1 (page 7) demonstrate state-of-the-art performance across multiple text-generation metrics. The model achieves BLEU-1 = 0.675, BLEU-2 = 0.585, BLEU-3 = 0.523, BLEU-4 = 0.472, METEOR = 0.382, ROUGE-1 = 0.72, ROUGE-2 = 0.55, and ROUGE-L = 0.698, outperforming prior transformer-based and CNN-based report generation methods such as ViGPT2 and CDGPT2. However, failure cases presented in Table 2 (page 8) reveal limitations in detecting rare or subtle findings, including omission of calcified granulomas and degenerative spinal changes, indicating dataset imbalance and underrepresentation of minority conditions. The model also exhibits difficulty in nuanced bony abnormality descriptions and generalization across unseen distributions. Despite strong generative performance, the framework does not explicitly incorporate imbalance-aware optimization, rare-class enhancement, or discriminative disease classification evaluation metrics such as AUC or F1-score. [6]
Wang et al. (2025), in the paper titled “ECAMP: Entity-centered Context-aware Medical Vision-Language Pre-training”, propose a novel medical vision-language pre-training framework designed to address two critical limitations in existing Med-VLP methods: linguistic complexity in radiology reports and severe descriptor imbalance between positive and negative disease expressions. The primary objective is to learn entity-centered and multi-scale contextual representations from paired medical image–report data. The proposed ECAMP framework integrates four synergistic components: (1) entity-aware context distillation using GPT-3.5 to simplify complex biomedical reports into structured diagnostic statements, (2) entity-centered context-enhanced masked language modeling (MLM) with descriptor masking and imbalance re-weighting, (3) context-guided super-resolution for lesion-focused reconstruction, and (4) multi-scale context fusion (MSCF) to simultaneously learn global and local visual representations. The model employs a ViT -B/16 backbone for the vision encoder, BERT-based text encoder/decoder, and masked image modeling (MIM) with 75% patch masking. A re-balancing factor ( λ_neg = 0.05) is introduced to mitigate the 20:1 negative-to-positive descriptor imbalance observed in MIMIC-CXR reports. Pre-training is conducted on MIMIC-CXR (377,110 images, 227,835 reports) and FFA-IR datasets. Extensive downstream evaluation across classification, segmentation, and detection tasks demonstrates state-of-the-art performance. On ChestX-ray14 fine-tuning classification (Table 4, p.8), ECAMP achieves AUC = 81.9% (1% data), 85.1% (10%), and 86.7% (100%), outperforming MRM and MGCA. On CheXpert , ECAMP reaches AUC = 89.7% (100%). For segmentation on SIIM (Table 6, p.9), Dice improves to 84.5% (100%), while RSNA detection achieves mAP = 31.6% (100%), surpassing prior ViT -based Med-VLP models. Notably, ECAMP shows strong generalization on COVIDx with ACC = 97.5% (100%), demonstrating improved novel-class separation capability. Ablation studies confirm that descriptor masking, re-balancing factors, super-resolution guidance, and MSCF collectively contribute to consistent gains across multi-scale tasks. Despite strong performance, limitations include dependence on LLM-based distillation accuracy, increased pre-training complexity, higher computational cost (4×A100 GPUs), and reliance on entity list curation (44 selected entities covering ~48.6% of report occurrences). [7]
Kishore et al. (2025), in the paper titled “Enhancing Medical Diagnosis on Chest X-rays: Knowledge Distillation from Self-Supervised Based Model to Compressed Student Model”, propose a knowledge distillation framework aimed at improving chest X-ray pneumonia classification while reducing computational complexity. The primary objective is to transfer knowledge from a large self-supervised teacher model (SWAV with ResNet-50 backbone) to a lightweight ResNet-18 student model for efficient deployment in resource-constrained environments. The methodology consists of three stages: (1) fine-tuning a pretrained SWAV self-supervised model on the Chest X-ray Pneumonia dataset (5,863 images), (2) applying teacher–student knowledge distillation using soft and hard targets, and (3) evaluating the compressed student model in terms of both diagnostic performance and computational efficiency. The loss function combines cross-entropy loss (hard labels) and Kullback – Leibler divergence (soft targets) with temperature scaling and weighted balancing. The dataset was reorganized using a 70:30 train–validation split. The student model (ResNet-18) achieved 97.34% accuracy, outperforming several prior CNN-based methods while significantly reducing model parameters and GFLOPs. The paper highlights improvements in precision, recall, and F1-score while demonstrating suitability for real-time deployment. However, the approach is limited to binary pneumonia vs. normal classification, does not address multi-disease imbalance issues, and does not incorporate multimodal vision-language reasoning. [8]
Zhang and Qie (2023), in the review article titled “Applying Deep Learning to Medical Imaging: A Review”, provide a comprehensive overview of deep learning techniques applied across various medical imaging modalities including MRI, CT, PET, ultrasound, and chest radiography. The objective of the study is to summarize core deep learning architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and analyze their roles in tasks such as image segmentation, classification, reconstruction, and registration. The paper discusses widely adopted architectures including ResNet , DenseNet , VGGNet , U-Net, LSTM, GRU, DCGAN, WGAN, and CycleGAN . It emphasizes transfer learning strategies using ImageNet-pretrained backbones and highlights evaluation metrics such as Accuracy, AUC, ROC curves, Dice coefficient, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. The review identifies key challenges including limited annotated datasets, class imbalance, model interpretability issues (“black-box” problem), adversarial vulnerability, and domain generalization. The authors conclude that while deep learning has revolutionized medical imaging diagnostics, future research must address explainability, robustness, data scarcity, and cross-modality generalization. [9]
Hsih et al. (2025), in the paper titled “Chest X-Ray Medical Report Generation Using a CNN–Transformer Model with Maximum Attention”, propose an automated image captioning framework to generate structured radiology reports from chest X-ray images. The primary objective is to reduce clinical workload by integrating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for feature extraction with a modified Transformer-based decoder referred to as the Medical Transformer. The architecture evaluates three CNN backbones—InceptionV3, ResNet152V2, and Inception–ResNetV2—for visual feature extraction. A novel “maximum attention” mechanism is introduced by experimenting with Bahdanau , Luong , and scaled dot-product attention variants activated via ReLU and Tanh functions to determine optimal alignment weights. Image augmentation is applied to enhance generalization. Experiments are conducted on the Indiana University Chest X-ray dataset (7,466 images with paired reports). Evaluation metrics include BLEU-1 to BLEU-4, METEOR, and BERTScore . The best configuration (Inception–ResNetV2 + Medical Transformer with maximum attention) achieves BLEU-1 = 0.720, BLEU-4 = 0.600, METEOR = 0.741, and BERTScore (F1) = 0.787, outperforming previous state-of-the-art captioning approaches (BLEU-1 ≈ 0.498). While the model demonstrates strong linguistic coherence and semantic similarity, limitations include reliance on reference-based metrics rather than diagnostic classification measures (AUC/F1), lack of imbalance-aware disease detection optimization, and computational overhead from attention experimentation. [10]
Ozturk et al. (2023), in the paper titled “ HydraViT : Adaptive Multi-Branch Transformer for Multi-Label Disease Classification from Chest X-ray Images”, propose a hybrid convolutional-transformer architecture designed to address multi-label thoracic disease classification and pathology co-occurrence modeling. The model integrates a CNN-based spatial encoder for local feature extraction with a transformer-based context encoder to capture long-range dependencies and inter-pathology relationships. A novel multi-branch output module assigns an independent output branch for each disease label alongside an aggregated branch capturing co-occurrence patterns. Adaptive weighting and a consistency loss function are introduced to balance label-specific sensitivity and co-occurrence awareness. Unlike traditional single-vector multi-label classifiers, HydraViT explicitly models individual and aggregated outputs to mitigate heterogeneous class performance. Experimental comparisons show that HydraViT outperforms attention-guided CNN methods by 1.2%, region-guided methods by 1.4%, and semantic-guided methods by 1.0% in multi-label classification performance. The framework demonstrates improved homogeneity across labels while maintaining contextual reasoning. However, limitations include increased architectural complexity, computational overhead from transformer blocks, and absence of multimodal vision-language supervision. [11]
Kim (2023), in the paper titled “ CheXFusion : Effective Fusion of Multi-View Features using Transformers for Long-Tailed Chest X-Ray Classification”, proposes a transformer-based fusion framework to address multi-label long-tailed thoracic disease classification from multi-view chest radiographs. The primary objective is to overcome challenges of class imbalance, label co-occurrence, and multi-view variability in the MIMIC-CXR dataset. The proposed CheXFusion framework consists of a two-stage architecture: (1) backbone pretraining using a ConvNeXt CNN with a transformer-based Ml-Decoder classification head, and (2) a transformer encoder-based fusion module that aggregates multi-view feature maps using self-attention and cross-attention mechanisms. The model incorporates positional encoding, learnable segment embeddings, feature shuffling, and a maximum image token limit (N=4). To address inter-class and intra-class imbalance, a hybrid loss combining weighted binary cross-entropy and asymmetric loss is employed. Additionally, Noisy Student self-training with soft pseudo-labeling is utilized to improve tail-class generalization. Experiments are conducted on the MIMIC-CXR-LT benchmark comprising 377,110 images across 26 disease categories. Evaluation metrics include mean Average Precision ( mAP ) and AUC-ROC, with performance reported across head, medium, and tail class groups. CheXFusion achieves a validation mAP of 0.372 and AUC of 0.847, outperforming multi-view weighted averaging (0.362 mAP ) and concatenation baselines. Tail-class mAP improved to 0.188, demonstrating effective handling of long-tailed distributions. On the official test set, the method achieved 0.372 mAP and 0.850 AUC, securing first place in the CXR-LT competition. Despite strong performance, limitations include frozen backbone training, lack of interpretability mechanisms, and absence of joint optimization between feature extractor and fusion module. [12]
